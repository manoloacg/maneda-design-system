Most people asking what it costs to design a house want one number. There isn't one, and anyone who gives you a number before they know the lot, the size of the house and what you want drawn is guessing. What you can know up front is how the fee is built, what it pays for, and what it leaves out. That is what this article covers. Where Maneda Design Studios (MDS) publishes its own prices, they are quoted here and on the [services page](/services/).

## What a design fee pays for

A design fee pays for decisions and the drawings that record them. On a new house or an addition, that work usually runs in phases:

- **Predesign.** The lot, the zoning, the setbacks, the flood zone and the local rules are reviewed before any design starts. The result is a program: the rooms, their sizes, and the limits the design has to work inside.
- **Schematic design.** Options are drawn and tested. At MDS this is usually three options, each with plans, elevations and massing studies, and you choose one.
- **Design development.** The chosen option is resolved: room sizes, openings, materials, finishes and fixtures.
- **Construction documents.** The design becomes the drawing set a builder prices from and the building department reviews for a permit.

Each phase has its own scope and its own fee. A designer who quotes one lump sum for "house plans" should be able to tell you which of these phases that sum includes.

## How design fees are usually structured

Residential design fees are usually set in one of three ways.

**A percentage of construction cost.** The fee is a share of what the house costs to build. It is common on larger projects. The weakness is that the construction cost is not known at the start, and the fee rises when the owner picks better windows or a bigger kitchen, even if the design work did not change. **Hourly.** The designer bills for time spent. It suits small or open-ended work, such as a consultation or a review of someone else's drawings. It is hard to budget for a whole house this way, because the hours depend on how many decisions change along the way.

**A fixed fee by phase.** The fee for each phase is set in writing before the phase starts. You know what the predesign costs, what schematic design costs, and so on. If the project stops after a phase, you have paid for that phase only. This is how MDS prices design work.

No method is right for every project. The point is to know which one you are signing, and what happens to the fee if the scope changes.

## Costs that are not part of the design fee

This is where budgets go wrong. A design fee is not the cost of getting a permit. Plan for these separately:

- **Structural engineering.** On many residential projects, a structural engineer prepares and seals the structural drawings. The owner hires the engineer under a separate contract.
- **Mechanical, electrical and plumbing engineering**, where the project or the jurisdiction requires it.
- **A boundary or topographic survey**, and soils tests when the site calls for them. Predesign tells you whether you need them.
- **Permit fees and impact fees**, set by the city or county. [VERIFY: if you want an example, cite the current fee schedule of one jurisdiction, with the date.]
- **Construction**, which is priced by your builder from the construction documents.

Ask every designer for a written list of what their fee does not include. If they cannot give you one, the gaps will show up later, at a worse time.

## What changes the fee

The same square footage can cost very different amounts to design. The main drivers are:

- **Scope.** Design only, or design through construction documents and permit.
- **Size and complexity.** More rooms, more levels, more custom details and more structural moves mean more drawing and more coordination.
- **The site.** A sloped lot, a flood zone, tight setbacks or an overlay district add review and design time.
- **Existing conditions.** Additions and renovations need the existing house measured and drawn before anything new is designed.
- **The jurisdiction.** Some building departments ask for more information on the drawings than others.

## How to keep design costs predictable

- **Start with the site.** A site review before design avoids designing a house the lot cannot hold.
- **Decide what you want drawn.** Design only and design through permit are different scopes with different fees.
- **Get the fee in writing, by phase**, with the deliverables for each phase listed.
- **Make decisions on time.** Most added cost comes from reopening decisions already made.
- **Budget the separate costs early**: engineer, survey, permit and impact fees.

## Questions to ask before you sign

1. Which phases does the fee include, and what do I receive at the end of each?
2. Is the fee fixed, hourly, or a percentage? What happens if the scope changes?
3. How many revision rounds are included, and what does an added round cost?
4. What is not included, and who provides it?
5. Who prepares and seals the structural drawings, and who hires them?
