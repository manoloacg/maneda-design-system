Most people asking what it costs to design a house want one number. It is a fair question. You are about to make one of the largest investments of your life, and you want to know what the first step will cost before you take it.

The honest answer is that a single number, given before anyone knows the lot, the size of the house and what you want drawn, is a guess. What you can know up front is how a design fee is built, what it pays for, what it leaves out, and what makes it rise or fall. With that, you can compare proposals clearly and avoid the most common budget surprise in residential projects: the costs nobody mentioned.

## What a design fee actually pays for

A design fee pays for decisions, and for the drawings that record them. On a new house, an addition or a major renovation, that work usually runs in phases.

It starts with research. The lot, zoning, setbacks, flood zone and local rules are reviewed before design begins, and the program is defined: the rooms, their sizes, how the family will use them, and the limits the design has to work inside.

Then comes concept design. Options are drawn and tested, each one a different answer to how the house sits, opens and connects. You choose a direction.

Design development follows. The chosen direction is resolved: room sizes, openings, proportions, materials, finishes and fixtures.

Finally, construction documents turn the design into the set a builder prices from and a building department reviews for a permit.

Each of these phases has its own scope, and each should have its own fee. If a proposal offers one lump sum for "house plans", ask which of these phases that sum includes. The difference between a design set and a full construction set is large, in effort and in what you can do with the result.

## How design fees are usually structured

Residential design fees are usually set in one of three ways.

**A percentage of construction cost.** The fee is a share of what the house costs to build. It is common on larger projects. Its weakness is that construction cost is unknown at the start, and the fee grows when you choose better windows or a larger kitchen, even if the design work did not change.

**Hourly.** The designer bills for time. It suits small or open-ended work, such as a consultation or a review of existing drawings. It is hard to budget for a whole house this way, because the hours depend on how many decisions change along the way.

**A fixed fee by phase.** The fee for each phase is set in writing before the phase begins. You know what the research costs, what concept design costs, and so on. If the project stops after a phase, you have paid for that phase only. This is how Maneda Design Studios prices design work.

No method is right for every project. What matters is that you know which one you are signing, and what happens to the fee if the scope changes.

## Costs that are not part of the design fee

This is where budgets usually go wrong. A design fee is not the cost of getting a permit, and it is not the cost of building. Plan for these separately from the start:

- **Structural engineering.** Many residential projects need structural drawings prepared and sealed by a structural engineer, usually hired by the owner under a separate contract.
- **Mechanical, electrical and plumbing engineering**, where the project or the jurisdiction requires it.
- **A boundary or topographic survey**, and soils tests when the site calls for them. Early research tells you whether you need them.
- **Permit fees and impact fees**, set by the city or county.
- **Construction**, priced by your builder from the construction documents.

Ask every designer for a written list of what their fee does not include. If they cannot give you one, the gaps will appear later, at a worse time.

## What makes a design fee rise or fall

The same square footage can cost very different amounts to design. The main drivers are:

- **Scope.** Design only, or design through construction documents and permit.
- **Size and complexity.** More rooms, more levels, more custom details and more structural moves mean more drawing and more coordination.
- **The site.** A sloped lot, a flood zone, tight setbacks or an overlay district add research and design time.
- **Existing conditions.** Additions and renovations need the existing house measured and drawn before anything new is designed.
- **The jurisdiction.** Some building departments ask for more on the drawings than others.

## The cost of a design that was too cheap

It is tempting to treat the design fee as the place to save. It is a small share of the total investment, and cutting it can look like easy money.

The risk sits on the other side. A design that skipped decisions does not make them disappear. It moves them to the construction site, where they are made quickly, under pressure, and at construction prices. A missing detail becomes a change order. A room that was never tested against the family's routine becomes a room that is rebuilt, or simply not used. Simplifying a project can look like savings at the start, but missing information tends to come back as doubt, delays and cost during construction.

A well-developed design does not remove every variable from a build. It does anticipate the important decisions, so you make them once, on paper, with time to think.

## How to keep design costs predictable

- **Start with the site.** Research before design avoids designing a house the lot cannot hold.
- **Decide what you want drawn.** Design only and design through permit are different scopes with different fees.
- **Get the fee in writing, by phase,** with the deliverables for each phase listed.
- **Make decisions on time.** Most added cost comes from reopening decisions already made.
- **Budget the separate costs early:** engineering, survey, permit and impact fees.

## Questions to ask before you sign

1. Which phases does the fee include, and what do I receive at the end of each?
2. Is the fee fixed, hourly, or a percentage? What happens if the scope changes?
3. How many revision rounds are included, and how are added rounds billed?
4. What is not included, and who provides it?
5. Who prepares and seals the structural drawings, and who hires them?
