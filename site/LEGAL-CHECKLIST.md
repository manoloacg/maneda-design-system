# Legal checklist: one review before launch

This is a list of what to ask a lawyer. It is not legal advice. One review of these items is cheap compared with fixing a problem later. Ask for a lawyer who works with design professionals or small service businesses. Because you take projects in more than one state, say so at the start.

When the review is done, set `"legalReviewDone": true` in `src/content/site.json`. The launch check then stops flagging it.

## 1. Your title and wording, by state
Research notes (2026-10-06, from search summaries and third-party legal sites, not the statutes themselves; verify with the lawyer):
- Florida protects the titles "architect," "registered architect," and "registered interior designer" (Fla. Stat. 481.223). Plans for one- and two-family residences do not require an architect (Fla. Stat. 481.229).
- California treats "architect," "architecture," and "architectural" as protected, and reports say "architectural designer" is prohibited for unlicensed people. Texas, Illinois, Nevada, and Florida are named as active enforcers.
- Ohio exempts non-architects who prepare residential plans (Ohio R.C. 4703.18) and bars holding out as an architect.

Because of this, the public site now says "Residential Designer" and "design studio". It no longer says "Architectural Designer". Ask: is "Residential Designer" safe in Florida and in every state where you take projects? May the site say you "trained in architecture" and hold degrees in architecture, as a plain statement of education? May the word "architectural" appear as a description of a drawing or service, such as "architectural drawings"? What can and cannot be said on a website, in a proposal, and on a title block? What wording may be used in search advertising and in a Google Business Profile category?

The site never calls you an architect and never says "licensed" about you. Ask the lawyer to confirm that is enough.

## 2. Your own documents
- Your meeting-minutes template lists you as "Designer / Architect of Record". Change it to "Designer". Ask the lawyer whether any past document that used that wording needs correcting.
- Check title blocks on drawing sets and permit sets for the same issue.

## 3. Your standard agreement
Ask the lawyer to review one standard agreement that covers:
- Scope by phase, two revision rounds, and added rounds billed hourly.
- Payment: half to start each phase, half on delivery, due in 7 days, and the next phase starting only after payment.
- Whether the deposit is refundable once work starts.
- The statement that services are provided by a designer and not a licensed architect, which your pricing guide already contains.
- That the structural engineer and other consultants are hired and paid by the owner under separate contracts.
- That drawings that need a seal are sealed by the licensed professional of record, not by you.
- Ownership of the drawings and permission to publish the project in your portfolio.
- Limits on your liability.
- What happens if the client stops responding, or cancels.
- Deposit rules in states where you take projects.

## 4. Permission to publish a project
Ask for a short written release you can send each client: they approve publishing the project, which images, and whether any name or address may appear. Get this in writing for the Ohio residence. A verbal OK is on file only.

## 5. The privacy policy
The policy on the site is a plain-language draft for a form-only site with no tracking cookies and no analytics. Ask the lawyer to confirm it is accurate for your set-up, including the third-party form service and the language setting stored in the browser. If you later turn on analytics, the policy must be updated first.

## 6. The seal sentence
"Where a seal is required, drawings are reviewed and sealed by a licensed professional of record." Ask whether this wording is right for projects outside Florida.

## 7. Insurance
Ask whether you should carry professional liability insurance, and what your agreement and website should say about it.
